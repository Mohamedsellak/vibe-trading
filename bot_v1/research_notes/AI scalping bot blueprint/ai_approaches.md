# AI / ML approaches for seconds-to-minutes trading and scalping: what works vs. hype (as of 2026)

Scope note: these notes cover model families, data/features, evaluation pitfalls and realistic expectations for a retail developer building a very-short-horizon (seconds to minutes) bot. Evidence is tagged as **[peer-reviewed]**, **[preprint]**, **[practitioner]**, or **[marketing/unverified]**. Date of research: 2026-11-16.

---

## Q1. Feature-based ML on microstructure features (OBI, OFI, microprice, etc.): does short-horizon predictive power exist, and does it pay after costs?

### Takeaway
Order-flow / order-book-imbalance features have the strongest peer-reviewed evidence of any short-horizon signal: OFI explains a large share of contemporaneous mid-price variation and deep-level OFI carries out-of-sample forecasting power for roughly "two average price changes" ahead. Every serious source, however, is explicit that mid-price predictability does not translate into net profit after spread, fees and latency, and no paper in this set reports net-of-cost retail-feasible profits from these features.

### Cited Findings
- Cont, Kukanov & Stoikov, "The Price Impact of Order Book Events", *Journal of Financial Econometrics* 12(1):47–88 (2014), using NYSE TAQ for 50 US stocks: finds a linear relation between order flow imbalance (OFI) and mid-price changes over short time scales, with slope inversely proportional to market depth; the working-paper version reports an average R² of ~65% (the search could not confirm this figure in the published version). **[peer-reviewed]** — [arXiv 1011.6402](https://arxiv.org/abs/1011.6402); [IDEAS/RePEc record](https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/jfinec/v12y2014i1p47-88..html)
- Stoikov, "The micro-price: a high-frequency estimator of future prices", *Quantitative Finance* 18(12):1959–1966 (2018): a Markov-chain estimator using quote imbalance and spread that predicts short-term prices better than the mid-price or weighted mid-price. **[peer-reviewed]** — [IDEAS record](https://ideas.repec.org/a/taf/quantf/v18y2018i12p1959-1966.html)
- Replication caveat: a Frankfurt master's thesis attempting to replicate the micro-price found that, contrary to Stoikov's results, the micro-price was not a better predictor of the future mid than the actual mid for most horizons. **[unreviewed thesis]** — [Goethe University thesis page](https://www.efinance.wiwi.uni-frankfurt.de/en/teaching/master-theses/selection-of-completed-theses/the-time-adjusted-micro-price-and-the-relevance-of-deeper-limit-order-book-levels.html)
- Kolm, Turiel & Westray, "Deep Order Flow Imbalance: Extracting Alpha at Multiple Horizons from the Limit Order Book", *Mathematical Finance* 33(4):1044–1081 (2023); 115 Nasdaq stocks, LOBSTER data Jan 2019–Jan 2020, models ARX/MLP/LSTM/LSTM-MLP/stacked LSTM/CNN-LSTM: models trained on multi-level order flow "significantly outperform most models trained directly on order book states"; stock-specific forecast accuracy peaks at about two average price changes and then decays; "information-rich" stocks (more book updates per price change) are forecast more accurately; the authors note that for HFT applications "trading costs must be considered" and report no net results. **[peer-reviewed]** — [SSRN 3900141](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=3900141); [RePEc record](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:bla:mathfi:v:33:y:2023:i:4:p:1044-1081); [Quantpedia summary](https://quantpedia.com/how-to-use-deep-order-flow-imbalance/)
- Sirignano & Cont, "Universal features of price formation in financial markets: perspectives from Deep Learning", *Quantitative Finance* 19(9):1449–1459 (2019): a single model pooled across all US stocks predicted out-of-sample better than stock-specific models, including on stocks absent from training; splitting by sector or tick size did not help; longer order-flow history improved forecasts (path dependence). **[peer-reviewed]** — [arXiv 1803.06917](https://arxiv.org/abs/1803.06917v1); [RePEc record](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:taf:quantf:v:19:y:2019:i:9:p:1449-1459)
- Lucchese, Pakkanen & Veraart, "The short-term predictability of returns in order book markets: a deep learning perspective", *International Journal of Forecasting* 40(4):1587–1621 (2024); 10 Nasdaq tickers (LOBSTER, Jan 2019–Jan 2020, 10 book levels, 11 five-week windows): mid-price predictability is "not just present, but ubiquitous"; for most stocks predictability is detectable up to ~50 order-book events ahead at 99% confidence (horizons measured in events, not seconds); order-flow (deepOF L2) and volume-representation models are in the superior model set 85–90% of the time vs. 0% for the naive benchmark and ~5–11% for the raw-state DeepLOB; L3 granularity gives no clear gain over L2; the paper reports no transaction-cost or profitability analysis. **[peer-reviewed]** — [arXiv 2211.13777](https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.13777v3); [RePEc record](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:eee:intfor:v:40:y:2024:i:4:p:1587-1621)
- Briola, Bartolucci & Aste, "Deep Limit Order Book Forecasting: A microstructural guide" (arXiv 2403.09267, 2024; 15 Nasdaq stocks): stock microstructural characteristics determine DL efficacy; "high forecasting power does not necessarily translate into actionable trading signals"; standard ML metrics fail to assess LOB forecast quality; released the open-source LOBFrame codebase. The follow-up HLOB paper notes small-tick stocks have sparser books that are harder for DL to exploit, large-tick stocks are easier. **[preprint]** — [arXiv 2403.09267](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.09267v4); [HLOB arXiv 2405.18938](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.18938v3)
- Aquilina, Budish & O'Neill (FCA Occasional Paper 50; *QJE* 137(1):493–564, 2022): latency-arbitrage races on the LSE occur about once per minute per FTSE-100 symbol, the modal race lasts 5–10 microseconds (mean ~79–81 µs), and each race is worth about half a tick. **[peer-reviewed]** — [FCA OP50](https://www.fca.org.uk/publications/occasional-papers/occasional-paper-no-50-quantifying-high-frequency-trading-arms-race-new-methodology); [NBER w29011](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w29011/w29011.pdf)

### Inferences
- The signal is real but its half-life is measured in order-book events / a couple of price changes, i.e. sub-second for liquid names. A retail bot with tens-to-hundreds of ms of round-trip latency observes a stale state; by the time an order arrives, much of the OFI-predicted move has been consumed by the microsecond-scale race documented by Aquilina et al.
- The practical retail use of OFI/OBI/microprice is therefore (a) as an entry-timing / queue-position filter for passive orders, (b) as a feature in a meta-model over a slower rule-based signal, and (c) on venues where the race is slower (crypto, less-liquid instruments), not as a standalone take-the-spread signal.
- Gradient boosting (LightGBM/XGBoost) on hand-built flow features is a reasonable retail choice: Kolm et al. and Lucchese et al. show that the *representation* (order flow / volume at levels) matters more than the architecture, and Sirignano & Cont show pooled training across instruments generalises. None of these papers evaluated GBDTs directly, so this is an inference, not a cited result.
- Large-tick instruments (deep, compact books: e.g. large-cap US equities, major FX pairs, BTC/ETH perps) are where imbalance features are most informative; small-tick / sparse-book instruments are the worst place to start.

### Gaps
- No paper found that reports net-of-cost, latency-aware P&L for OFI/OBI-based strategies executable by a retail participant. Every peer-reviewed source stops at prediction quality.
- Could not verify the 65% R² figure in the published 2014 version of Cont et al. (only in the working paper).
- No peer-reviewed benchmarks found of LightGBM/XGBoost/CatBoost specifically on LOB features vs. deep models at these horizons.

## Q2. Deep learning (LSTM/GRU, temporal CNNs, DeepLOB and successors, transformers, time-series foundation models): out-of-sample edge vs. overfitting

### Takeaway
DeepLOB-style models do measurably beat naive baselines at classifying the next few mid-price moves, but every controlled study shows the edge shrinks dramatically or turns negative once even tiny costs are applied; transformers do not beat LSTM/CNN on LOB data, and time-series foundation models (Chronos, TimesFM, Moirai, Lag-Llama) show no reliable alpha and are often beaten by naive/ARIMA baselines at short horizons.

### Cited Findings
- Zhang, Zohren & Roberts, "DeepLOB: Deep Convolutional Neural Networks for Limit Order Books" (*IEEE Trans. Signal Processing* 2019; arXiv 1808.03668): the trading simulation uses mid-prices with no transaction costs; the authors concede that careful entry timing and more elaborate rules would be needed to cover costs; out-of-sample evidence is prediction accuracy over a ~3-month test period. **[peer-reviewed]** — [arXiv 1808.03668](https://arxiv.org/abs/1808.03668)
- Bilokon & Qiu, "Transformers versus LSTMs for electronic trading" (arXiv 2309.11400, Imperial College, 2023), Binance BTC-USDT / ETH-USDT LOB, July 2022: on 3-class movement prediction their DLSTM reached 73.1% (k=20 events) to 63.7% (k=100) accuracy; all out-of-sample R² for absolute mid-price regression were negative ("not useful for trading"); in simulation with execution at mid and no impact, applying a 0.002% cost cut cumulative return for LSTM from 15.4 to 2.1 (k=20), for DeepLOB from 13.9 to 2.0, for the vanilla Transformer from 14.6 to 1.9, and pushed Autoformer negative at k=50/100 and Transformer Sharpe to −23.5 at k=100; the authors themselves call the annualised Sharpes "enormous" and the assumptions "not realistic for practical trading"; LSTM-family models were more robust to cost than transformer-family models. **[preprint]** — [arXiv 2309.11400](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2309.11400)
- Lucchese et al. (2024, see Q1): raw-state DeepLOB appeared in the superior model set only 5–11% of the time vs. 85–90% for order-flow / volume-representation inputs — the *input representation*, not depth of network, drove results. **[peer-reviewed]** — [arXiv 2211.13777](https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.13777v3)
- Briola et al. (2024): "high forecasting power does not necessarily translate into actionable trading signals"; DeepLOB performance depends heavily on the stock's microstructure. **[preprint]** — [arXiv 2403.09267](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.09267v4)
- "LSTM beats everything" critique: a 2025 CSI-300 study found the LSTM's advantage over a random-walk benchmark was not statistically significant under a Diebold–Mariano test; a student project found a naive persistence baseline beat the LSTM on RMSE/MAE; a secondary write-up describes the "predictive lag illusion" where high-accuracy LSTM price plots are just the actual series shifted one step. **[mixed: peer-reviewed / student / secondary]** — [Springer Financial Innovation 2025](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40854-025-00875-9); [Edinburgh UG4 project](https://project-archive.inf.ed.ac.uk/ug4/20244184/ug4_proj.pdf); [mental-momentum.ai write-up](https://research.mental-momentum.ai/r/lstm-recurrent-networks-stock-price-6rwg87)
- Time-series foundation models: a 2026 arXiv benchmark on five US stocks concluded TSFMs (Moirai-2.0, TimesFM-2.5, Chronos) "serve as useful practical priors that reduce model-development costs in low-data financial forecasting, but are not universal engines for statistically reliable alpha generation". **[preprint]** — [arXiv 2606.27100 (RePEc)](https://econpapers.repec.org/paper/arxpapers/2606.27100.htm)
- A cross-sectional excess-return study found off-the-shelf Chronos-Large and TimesFM-500M "perform poorly on financial forecasting tasks" and that "domain-agnostic pre-training is insufficient"; performance is highly sensitive to tokenizer/architecture hyperparameters. **[preprint]** — [arXiv 2511.18578](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2511.18578)
- Single-stock MSFT study: naive and ARIMA remain strongest for short-horizon price forecasts; foundation models only help at longer horizons / short training windows. **[preprint]** — [arXiv 2507.07296](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.07296v1)
- A 2026 Chronos-2 study found cross-series information can help, but mixing equity and rate series reduces accuracy. **[preprint]** — [arXiv 2605.21504 (RePEc)](https://econpapers.repec.org/paper/arxpapers/2605.21504.htm)

### Inferences
- The right mental model: DL on LOB data learns a slightly better *microprice* — a nowcast of where the mid is heading over the next handful of events. That is a market-maker's / execution desk's tool, not a retail directional scalping signal.
- The 0.002% cost used by Bilokon & Qiu is roughly 10–50× smaller than what a retail crypto taker pays (typical taker fees are a few bp) and ignores spread and queue position; since even that cost removed ~85% of simulated return, a retail taker strategy built on DeepLOB-class predictions would be expected to lose money. (The fee comparison is my inference; the paper's cost figure is cited.)
- Transformers and TSFMs are not worth retail effort for scalping as of 2026: no study shows them beating LSTM/CNN on LOB data net of costs, and they are more fragile to costs. TSFMs may be useful as cheap volatility/volume *priors* (e.g. forecasting intraday volume or realised vol for sizing), not for direction.
- Architecture choice matters far less than (a) using stationary flow/volume representations, (b) honest cost modelling, (c) latency-aware labelling.

### Gaps
- No study found that evaluates DeepLOB-class models with realistic retail fees (maker/taker bp), queue position and ≥50 ms latency on live or replayed data.
- No peer-reviewed evaluation of TimesFM/Chronos/Moirai/Lag-Llama at seconds-to-minutes horizons on LOB data; all TSFM evidence found is daily-frequency.
- The Medium/YouTube "LSTM predicts price" genre could not be cited to a single representative article; the critiques above are the best available proxy.

## Q3. Reinforcement learning: what the evidence shows and what is realistic for retail

### Takeaway
RL has credible academic and (press-reported) industry use in *execution optimisation* and *market making*, where the reward is well-defined and the simulator is close to reality; as a *signal-generation* engine for retail directional trading the literature itself admits widespread backtest overfitting, hyperparameter-selection-on-test, reproducibility failures and a sim-to-real gap, and there is no independent evidence of live retail success.

### Cited Findings
- Zhang, Zohren & Roberts, "Deep Reinforcement Learning for Trading", *Journal of Financial Data Science* 2(2) (Spring 2020; arXiv 1911.10107): DQN/PG/A2C learn positions directly on continuous futures with volatility-scaled rewards; a secondary summary says DRL beat momentum baselines on annualised return and Sharpe — but this is daily-frequency futures, not scalping. **[peer-reviewed; results via secondary source]** — [arXiv 1911.10107](https://arxiv.org/abs/1911.10107v1)
- Gašperov et al., "Reinforcement Learning Approaches to Optimal Market Making", *Mathematics* 9(21):2689 (2021): review concludes RL market makers achieve superior risk-adjusted return vs. analytical (Avellaneda–Stoikov-type) strategies, in simulation. **[peer-reviewed review]** — [MDPI](https://mdpi.com/2227-7390/9/21/2689)
- Bai et al., "A Review of Reinforcement Learning in Financial Applications" (arXiv 2411.12746, 2024): a DQN agent can outperform the Avellaneda–Stoikov PDE baseline; adversarial multi-agent MM variants exist (Gašperov & Kostanjčar 2021). **[preprint review]** — [arXiv 2411.12746](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2411.12746)
- Nagy, Calliess & Zohren (Oxford-Man Institute, 2023), "Asynchronous Deep Double Duelling Q-learning for trading-signal execution": an RL agent on an ABIDES-based gym translates a high-frequency signal into limit orders on NASDAQ AAPL data and beats a baseline — an *execution* task, not signal generation. **[preprint]** — [Synced write-up](https://syncedreview.com/2023/01/25/oxford-us-deep-double-duelling-q-learning-translates-trading-signals-into-sota-trading-strategies/amp/); related JAX-LOB simulator [arXiv 2308.13289](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2308.13289)
- Industry use: the only evidence that JPMorgan uses RL for execution is second-hand (a 2020 FAU paper citing 2017 press reports); no JPMorgan performance data is public. **[practitioner/press]** — [FAU working paper](https://www.iwf.rw.fau.de/files/2020/04/05_2020.pdf)
- Schnaubelt (2020/2022) compared deep-RL algorithms vs. benchmarks for optimal placement of *cryptocurrency* limit orders; Cheridito & Weiss (ETH Zurich, 2025, arXiv 2507.06345) use RL to choose market vs. limit orders and report outperformance in numerical experiments. **[preprint]** — [arXiv 2507.06345](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.06345v2)
- FinRL ecosystem self-critique: the FinRL-Meta paper lists "model overfitting in the backtesting stage" as a core difficulty; a FinRL-affiliated crypto paper states prior DRL works "optimistically reported increased profits in backtesting, which may suffer from the false positive issue due to model overfitting" and "gave the false impression that DRL-based trading strategies may be ready to deploy", noting FinRL-Podracer selected the best-return agent across hyperparameters as its reported result. **[preprint]** — [FinRL-Meta arXiv 2211.03107](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2211.03107); [arXiv 2209.05559](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2209.05559)
- Reproducibility: FinRL Contests paper says lack of standardised tasks, datasets, environments and baselines "has hindered consistent evaluation and reproducibility". **[preprint]** — [arXiv 2504.02281](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.02281v4)
- Surveys: Pippas et al. (ACM, 167 papers) name non-stationarity and noise as core problems; Sun, Wang & An (2023) list non-stationarity, partial observability, reward shaping and sim-to-real transfer as open challenges; the general sim-to-real survey (Da et al., arXiv 2502.13187, 2025) is not finance-specific. **[preprint/ACM]** — [arXiv 2408.10932](https://arxiv.org/html/2408.10932v3); [arXiv 2512.10913](https://www.arxiv.org/pdf/2512.10913); [arXiv 2502.13187](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.13187v3)
- Anecdotal sim-to-real gap: on the ai4finance forum a user's FX agent looked strong on training data and a maintainer attributed the live failure to the "simulation-to-reality gap". **[practitioner anecdote]** — [ai4finance Google Group](https://groups.google.com/g/ai4finance/c/XADOEvkGwEc)

### Inferences
- Where RL is legitimately used (execution, market making), the agent's *environment* is largely known: the firm's own fills, queue dynamics and inventory. The reward (implementation shortfall, inventory-penalised P&L) is dense and well-specified. Directional signal generation has none of these properties — the reward is dominated by noise, so RL mostly learns the backtest.
- For retail, RL only makes sense as a thin *policy layer* on top of a validated signal (e.g. deciding limit vs. market, offset from touch, when to cancel) in a simulator that models your own latency and fill probability; even then expect a sim-to-real gap because historical replay cannot model how the market reacts to your orders.
- Reward hacking and sample inefficiency are compounded at short horizons: a bot that learns to earn maker rebates by sitting in the queue will be adversely selected live; a bot trained on one regime of a few weeks of ticks has no generalisation guarantee.

### Gaps
- No independent, published live-trading result for any RL retail trading bot at seconds-to-minutes horizons was found.
- Exact performance tables for Zhang et al. (2020) and Gašperov et al. (2021) were not retrieved (abstract/secondary only).

## Q4. LLM-based approaches (FinGPT, FinBERT, FinAgent, TradingAgents, FinMem) and 2025–2026 evaluations

### Takeaway
The 2025–2026 benchmark literature is consistently negative or at best mixed: most LLM trading agents fail to beat buy-and-hold, reasoning-benchmark scores do not predict trading results, "gross" alpha disappears after costs, and a May-2026 position paper argues reported LLM-agent alpha should not be treated as deployment evidence. All LLM agent evaluations found operate at *daily* decision frequency; nothing supports LLMs in the sub-second execution path. Their defensible roles are offline: news/event filtering, regime labelling, research and code assistance.

### Cited Findings
- StockBench (Chen et al., arXiv 2510.02209, v2 March 2026): contamination-free, multi-month environment, agents receive *daily* prices/fundamentals/news and make buy/sell/hold decisions; "most models struggle to outperform the simple buy-and-hold baseline"; strong financial-QA performance "does not necessarily translate into effective trading"; performance declined as the universe grew from 5 to 30 stocks. **[preprint]** — [arXiv 2510.02209](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.02209)
- LiveTradeBench (arXiv 2511.03628, 2025): high general-purpose LLM reasoning scores "do not correlate with superior trading outcomes". **[preprint]** — [emergentmind summary of 2511.03628](https://www.emergentmind.com/papers/2511.03628)
- FINSABER (arXiv 2505.07078, accepted KDD 2026): over a 20-year backtest, LLM strategies "underperform in bull markets due to excessive conservatism and suffer disproportionate losses in bear markets". **[accepted/peer-reviewed]** — [arXiv 2505.07078](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2505.07078v4)
- Alpha Arena (nof1.ai, Oct–Nov 2025, live, $10k per model): organisers concluded AI agents "are not really good at trading" under Season-1 conditions. **[practitioner/live, promotional context]** — [aiworld.eu summary](https://aiworld.eu/story/ai-agents-against-the-market)
- Ye et al., "The Alpha Illusion: Reported Alpha from LLM Trading Agents Should Not Be Treated as Deployment Evidence" (arXiv 2605.16895, 16 May 2026): position paper arguing public evidence cannot separate predictive ability from temporal contamination, unmodelled frictions, short-window Sharpe uncertainty, narrative fitting and parametric priors; "language confidence isn't a tradable probability, narrative reasoning isn't numerical execution"; proposes a P1–P6 reporting protocol and recommends LLMs as "auditable information interfaces feeding independent calibration, risk, and execution modules". Names FinCon, FinMem, TradingAgents, FinAgent, QuantAgent, FLAG-Trader as the ecosystem in question. A related overview reports reproductions of TradingAgents and QuantAgent where gross returns turn into net losses or underperformance vs. buy-and-hold after realistic costs. **[preprint, position paper]** — [arXiv 2605.16895](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.16895); [alphaxiv overview](https://www.alphaxiv.org/overview/2605.16895)
- "Win Fast or Lose Slow" (arXiv 2505.19481, 2025): notes FinMem (Yu et al. 2023) and FinAgent (Zhang et al. 2024) were "evaluated on static historical datasets and ignore the role of response timing, which is crucial in real-time trading"; HFT is named as a strict-latency setting; sacrificing quality for lower latency improved their simulated daily yield by up to 26.5%. **[preprint]** — [arXiv 2505.19481](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2505.19481)
- Low-quality items to ignore: a Reddit-reposted "90-day benchmark" claiming expensive LLMs underperform cheaper ones has no verifiable methodology; a KuCoin news flash citing a 33% portfolio decline across 32 AI systems gives no source. **[marketing/unverified]** — [aiweekly repost](https://aiweekly.co/node/2535); [KuCoin flash](https://www.kucoin.com/news/flash/ai-trading-models-struggle-in-live-market-tests-majority-of-systems-report-losses)

### Inferences
- A scalping decision loop needs sub-100 ms (ideally sub-10 ms) reaction; a hosted LLM call is of order 1–10 s plus network, i.e. 2–3 orders of magnitude too slow, and non-deterministic. This latency figure is my inference from general LLM serving behaviour; no source in this set measured LLM latency against a trading deadline.
- Where LLMs can add value for a retail scalper: (1) classifying scheduled-news / event risk windows to *switch the bot off* (a filter, computed minutes ahead); (2) labelling regimes or tagging headlines into a feature that is refreshed every few minutes; (3) writing/reviewing feature code, backtests and reports; (4) FinBERT-style sentiment as a slow exogenous feature — but none of the sources above shows sentiment improving seconds-to-minutes direction.
- The "LLM trading agent beats the market" claims on YouTube/Medium are, as of 2026, contradicted by every controlled benchmark found.

### Gaps
- No source measured LLM inference latency explicitly against scalping deadlines; the unsuitability argument rests on the daily-frequency design of all benchmarks plus general latency knowledge.
- No evaluation found of FinBERT/FinGPT sentiment as a feature at intraday horizons with costs.

## Q5. Hybrid rule-based + ML (meta-labelling, triple-barrier, purged CV, bet sizing, HMM regimes): why this is most realistic for retail

### Takeaway
Meta-labelling (ML as a filter/sizer over a simple primary signal) is the most defensible retail architecture because it attacks the two things that actually kill retail bots — false positives and oversizing — rather than trying to forecast direction; the supporting evidence is mostly from parties with a commercial interest (Hudson & Thames) and one independent test found no benefit over an end-to-end model, so expect modest, not transformative, gains. HMM regime filters are popular but the best-controlled evidence finds a 200-day MA does as well.

### Cited Findings
- Hudson & Thames research (March 2019 update; "Does meta-labeling add to signal efficacy?"): claims that event-based sampling + triple-barrier + meta-labelling improved trend-following and mean-reversion strategies on S&P 500 E-mini out-of-sample; the toy MNIST example shows out-of-sample metric improvement. Hudson & Thames sells MLFinLab, so the evidence is from an interested party. **[practitioner, commercial]** — [Hudson & Thames: meta-labeling](https://hudsonthames.org/does-meta-labeling-add-to-signal-efficacy/); [toy example](https://hudsonthames.org/meta-labeling-a-toy-example/)
- Independent pushback: a QuantConnect forum grid search found "the meta-model is not able to squeeze additional returns compared with a single end-to-end model" and that meta-labelling helps most when the primary model is simple (linear or discretionary). **[practitioner]** — [QuantConnect: why meta labeling is not a silver bullet](https://www.quantconnect.com/forum/discussion/14706/why-meta-labeling-is-not-a-silver-bullet/)
- Bailey, Borwein, López de Prado & Zhu, "The Probability of Backtest Overfitting", *Journal of Computational Finance* (2015/2017): CSCV framework estimates how likely a backtest's in-sample winner underperforms out-of-sample; authors note CSCV's block reshuffling weakens temporal ordering so it complements rather than replaces walk-forward. **[peer-reviewed]** — [SSRN 2326253](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=2326253); [eScholarship](https://escholarship.org/content/qt4w1110bb/qt4w1110bb.pdf)
- Bailey & López de Prado, "The Deflated Sharpe Ratio" (2014): corrects Sharpe for selection bias from multiple testing and non-normality. **[peer-reviewed]** — [SSRN 2460551](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=2460551)
- Robot Wealth (Kris Longmore): "With machine learning, we can test thousands of models and will certainly find something that looks good"; argues correcting for data-mining bias (White's Reality Check) "makes ALL the difference"; "identify your edge first"; "portfolio beats prediction"; short-term results "utterly dominated by randomness". **[practitioner]** — [Robot Wealth ML framework slides](https://www.slideshare.net/slideshow/a-framework-for-developing-trading-models-based-on-machine-learning-by-kris-longmore-founder-of-robotwealth/68740886); [Robot Wealth on Medallion/expectations](https://robotwealth.com/renaissance-medallion-performance/); [How do I know if I have an edge?](https://robotwealth.com/2021/04/02)
- Ernest Chan: stresses simple, causal, interpretable models, out-of-sample testing and live experience; his firm's ML work focuses on *predicting the profitability of a strategy's trades* (meta-labelling by another name) and risk management rather than price direction. **[practitioner]** — [IBKR Campus: Chan on ML in risk management](https://www.interactivebrokers.com/campus/ibkr-quant-news/algo-advantage-032-dr-ernest-chan-the-breakthrough-uses-of-machine-learning-in-risk-management/); [Better System Trader ep.192](https://bettersystemtrader.libsyn.com/192-predicting-profitability-using-machine-learning-ernie-chan)
- HMM regime detection: a summarised study over 31 assets / 2,700 backtests reports the regime effect is real in aggregate but "no single config survives correction" and "the simplest regime detector (200-day moving average) performed as well as or better than the complex HMM". Quantpedia warns that selecting HMMs by out-of-sample likelihood is misleading; a 2024 paper proposes statistical jump models to fix HMM state-persistence and small-sample problems. **[secondary summary / practitioner / preprint]** — [SozAI transcript](https://sozai.app/transcript/testing-market-regimes-trading-strategies/index.md); [Quantpedia HMM](https://quantpedia.com/does-more-history-help-a-market-regime-hmm-and-the-trouble-with-out-of-sample-evaluation/); [arXiv 2402.05272](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2402.05272v1.pdf)

### Inferences
- The retail-realistic stack: (1) a transparent rule-based scalping trigger with a known, explainable reason to exist (e.g. mean reversion after liquidity sweep, imbalance-confirmed breakout at session open); (2) a binary meta-model (LightGBM/logistic) that predicts "will this trigger's trade hit TP before SL within the time barrier"; (3) bet size = f(meta-probability) with a cap; (4) a *simple* regime/volatility gate (realised vol bucket, time-of-day, spread-width threshold) rather than a tuned HMM. This directly uses the only two things ML has evidence for at these horizons: filtering false positives and sizing.
- Evaluation must be event-based with purging and embargo (labels at short horizons overlap heavily), and reported with PBO/DSR so a 55%-precision meta-model is not mistaken for an edge after hundreds of trials.
- Fractional differentiation is a Lopez de Prado preprocessing idea; no source in this set demonstrates it matters at seconds horizons, where returns/flow features are already near-stationary. Treat as optional.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed, independent out-of-sample validation of meta-labelling at intraday/scalping horizons was found; evidence is practitioner-level and partly commercial.
- The original "2,700 backtests" HMM study was only seen as a video transcript summary; the primary source was not located.

## Q6. Data and feature requirements, labelling at short horizons, leakage and latency-aware labels

### Takeaway
Seconds-to-minutes ML needs event-level data with bid/ask (and ideally several book levels); the literature agrees flow/volume representations at L2 are enough (L3 adds little), that horizons should be measured in events or price changes rather than clock time, and that models must be trained on information available at decision time plus execution delay. Crypto provides this for free; equities require paid feeds (Databento MBP-10/MBO); retail FX typically exposes only one broker's top-of-book and tick-count "volume".

### Cited Findings
- Databento organises and prices data by level: L1 (top of book, MBP-1), L2 (depth, MBP-10), L3 (full order-by-order, MBO); usage-based pay-as-you-go or subscription; a third-party forum post quotes ~$2/GB PAYG or a $179/month standard plan (unverified against the official pricing page); no free MBO tier found. **[vendor docs / practitioner]** — [Databento MBO schema](https://databento.com/docs/schemas-and-data-formats/mbo); [Databento MBP-10](https://databento.com/docs/schemas-and-data-formats/mbp-10); [NexusFi review](https://nexusfi.com/a/data/databento-futures-market-data)
- Binance publishes public WebSocket depth streams (partial book 5/10/20 levels; diff streams at 100 ms or 1000 ms) with a documented snapshot + buffered-diff procedure for maintaining a local book; the docs note Retail Price Improvement (RPI) orders are not visible, so the public book is not the full resting liquidity. **[vendor docs]** — [Binance: manage a local order book](https://developers.binance.com/docs/derivatives/usds-margined-futures/websocket-market-streams/How-to-manage-a-local-order-book-correctly); [Binance SBE market data streams](https://developers.binance.com/en/docs/products/spot/sbe-market-data-streams)
- Lucchese et al. (2024): L3 granularity gave no clear gain over L2 ("excessively granular"); horizons measured in order-book events; predictability persistence correlates with updates-per-price-change, suggesting a *price-change clock* gives more consistent horizons across stocks. **[peer-reviewed]** — [arXiv 2211.13777](https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.13777v3)
- Kolm et al. (2023): LOBSTER order-book events time-stamped to nanoseconds; best forecast horizon ≈ two average price changes; order-flow inputs (stationary) beat raw book states. **[peer-reviewed]** — [Quantpedia summary](https://quantpedia.com/how-to-use-deep-order-flow-imbalance/)
- Sirignano & Cont (2019): a pooled cross-asset model generalises to unseen stocks; standard normalisations and tick-size splits did not help; longer histories helped. **[peer-reviewed]** — [arXiv 1803.06917](https://arxiv.org/abs/1803.06917v1)
- "Lag illusion" / leakage sources: temporal data leakage, survivorship bias and random (non-chronological) cross-validation are listed as common failure modes in LSTM price-prediction write-ups. **[secondary]** — [mental-momentum.ai](https://research.mental-momentum.ai/r/lstm-recurrent-networks-stock-price-6rwg87)
- Latency reality for equities: races last 5–10 µs modally and ~80 µs on average (Aquilina et al. 2022). **[peer-reviewed]** — [NBER w29011](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w29011/w29011.pdf)

### Inferences (and the concrete recommended first-model spec)
- **Instrument choice**: start with a large-tick, deep-book, 24/7 instrument with free L2 data — BTC-USDT or ETH-USDT perpetuals — because (a) full depth is free, (b) books are compact (DL/flow features work best, per HLOB/Briola), (c) you can replay your own recorded stream rather than buying history. US equities are the second choice (Databento MBP-10, paid). Retail FX is the worst starting point: single-broker top-of-book, no real volume (tick counts only), and dealer-side last-look. (The FX specifics are not sourced in this research — see Gaps.)
- **Data to record from day one** (your own capture is the only way to get latency-faithful data): every L2 update (10 levels each side), every trade with aggressor side, your own order acks/fills with local timestamps, and the exchange timestamp — so you can measure your actual feed-to-exchange latency distribution.
- **Recommended feature list (first retail model)**, all computed at decision time t using only data with exchange timestamp ≤ t − (your measured feed latency):
  1. Level-1 order-book imbalance: (Qbid − Qask)/(Qbid + Qask), plus the same for cumulative depth over 3 and 5 levels.
  2. Multi-level OFI (Cont et al. 2014 definition, generalised per Kolm et al. 2023) summed over the last 10, 50, 200 events.
  3. Microprice minus mid, in ticks (Stoikov 2018), and its change over the last N events.
  4. Trade-flow imbalance: signed aggressor volume over last 1 s / 5 s / 30 s, and its z-score vs. the trailing hour.
  5. Spread in ticks and spread z-score; a binary "spread > 1 tick" flag (do not trade when wide).
  6. Realised volatility over the last 1 min / 5 min (mid returns), and vol bucket (tercile vs. trailing day).
  7. Recent mid returns over last 10, 50, 200 events and last 1 / 5 min; deviation of mid from session VWAP and from 5-min VWAP, in ticks.
  8. Book-update rate (events per second) and trades per second over the last 10 s — the "information-rich vs. quiet" proxy from Kolm et al.
  9. Time-of-day (cyclic encoding), day-of-week, minutes-to/from scheduled macro releases and funding timestamps (crypto), session flag (Asia/London/NY).
  10. Queue-position proxy for passive entries: size ahead at your intended price level.
  Keep ≤ 25 features; use LightGBM with monotone constraints where sensible; pooled training across BTC and ETH (Sirignano & Cont) is reasonable.
- **Labelling scheme (first model)**: meta-label a rule-based trigger, not raw direction. For each trigger event at t: assume entry fills at t + Δ (Δ = your measured p90 order latency, e.g. 150–300 ms for a cloud-hosted retail box) at the then-prevailing *ask for buys / bid for sells* (taker) or at your limit only if the book traded through it (maker); then apply a triple barrier: TP = +k·ATR_1min, SL = −k·ATR_1min, time barrier = either N price changes (Lucchese/Kolm recommend a price-change clock) or a fixed 30–120 s; label = 1 if TP hit first *net of fees and half-spread*, else 0. Fees must be the actual taker/maker bp of your account tier. Report label base rate; expect heavy imbalance and consider sample weights by label uniqueness (overlapping barriers). Use purged, embargoed walk-forward CV with the embargo ≥ the longest barrier.
- **Evaluation**: metric is net expectancy per trade in ticks and PBO/DSR across all configurations tried, not accuracy. Log the number of experiments run.

### Gaps
- No authoritative source retrieved on retail FX data limitations (top-of-book only, tick-volume only, last-look); this is stated as inference and should be verified against specific broker/API documentation.
- Databento's official pricing page was not fetched; the $/GB and $/month figures are third-party and may be stale.
- No source found that quantifies typical retail cloud-to-exchange latency for crypto venues; the 150–300 ms figure above is an assumption to be measured, not a cited fact.

## Q7. Realistic expectations: out-of-sample accuracy/Sharpe, edge decay, why retail ML scalping fails

### Takeaway
The honest evidence base says: short-horizon mid-price direction can be classified somewhat above chance (the only cost-aware study found ~64–73% three-class accuracy on crypto LOB, collapsing to near-zero or negative P&L under a 0.002% cost), LLM agents mostly lose to buy-and-hold, RL results are self-admittedly overfit, and practitioner sources agree the dominant failure modes are data-mining bias, unmodelled costs and non-stationarity. No reputable source promises a positive expected return for retail ML scalping.

### Cited Findings
- Bilokon & Qiu (2023): accuracy 73.1% (k=20) → 63.7% (k=100) for DLSTM on ETH-USDT 3-class movement; a 0.002% cost removed ~85% of simulated cumulative return for the best models and turned transformer-family models negative; authors state the simulated Sharpes are unrealistic. **[preprint]** — [arXiv 2309.11400](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2309.11400)
- Lucchese et al. (2024): predictability persists only ~50 events ahead for most stocks and is absent for the most illiquid one (LILAK); no accuracy or P&L figures reported, only model-confidence-set membership. **[peer-reviewed]** — [arXiv 2211.13777](https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.13777v3)
- Kolm et al. (2023): stock-level alpha peaks at ~two price changes ahead and declines after — i.e. the signal decays within the time a retail order is in flight. **[peer-reviewed]** — [Quantpedia summary](https://quantpedia.com/how-to-use-deep-order-flow-imbalance/)
- StockBench 2026: "most models struggle to outperform the simple buy-and-hold baseline". **[preprint]** — [arXiv 2510.02209](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.02209)
- FinRL-affiliated authors: prior DRL trading papers "gave the false impression that DRL-based trading strategies may be ready to deploy". **[preprint]** — [arXiv 2209.05559](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2209.05559)
- Robot Wealth: "you have to get rid of unrealistic expectations of a certain, steadily increasing equity curve"; good trading "feels dumb" because short-term results are dominated by randomness; some edges lose money for months; thousands of ML models tested will "certainly find something that looks good". **[practitioner]** — [Robot Wealth](https://robotwealth.com/renaissance-medallion-performance/); [slides](https://www.slideshare.net/slideshow/a-framework-for-developing-trading-models-based-on-machine-learning-by-kris-longmore-founder-of-robotwealth/68740886)
- Bailey et al. / López de Prado: the probability of backtest overfitting rises with the number of trials; the Deflated Sharpe Ratio exists precisely because the best of many backtests has an inflated Sharpe under zero skill. **[peer-reviewed]** — [SSRN 2326253](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=2326253); [SSRN 2460551](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=2460551)
- Market-making side: a 2025 arXiv study of Binance BTC perpetuals frames MM as fill-probability vs. post-fill return and notes a top-of-book order fills "with probability 1" when the next move goes against it (adverse selection); a retail-oriented post says maker rebates are needed to be profitable and even then margins are "razor-thin", with inventory risk the main killer; the Hummingbot liquidity-mining paper says exchanges/issuers spend ~$1.2bn/yr compensating market makers. An "89% of retail MM bots lose money in 3 months" figure found online has no methodology. **[preprint / practitioner / marketing-unverified]** — [arXiv 2502.18625](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.18625v2); [dev.to post](https://dev.to/trendrider/crypto-market-making-bots-how-they-actually-work-and-why-most-fail-2kn); [Hummingbot liquidity mining paper](https://docs.hummingbot.org/liquidity-mining.pdf)

### Inferences
- Translate the literature into retail numbers: a meta-model that lifts the precision of a rule-based trigger from, say, 50% to 54–56% *on net-of-cost labels* is the realistic ceiling to aim for; the headline 70%+ accuracies in LOB papers are on 3-class event-horizon labels with mid-price execution and vanish under costs. (The 54–56% band is my synthesis; no source states a canonical "51–55%" figure — see Gaps.)
- Expectancy, not accuracy, is the target: with a 1:1 TP/SL in ATR units, breaking even after 2–4 bp of fees+spread on a 10–20 bp target already requires ~55% precision; most retail "wins" at 60%+ in backtests are either leaking future data or ignoring the spread.
- Edge decays for structural reasons: the signal half-life is a few price changes; faster participants arbitrate it first; and any retail-visible pattern is, by construction, visible to firms with better latency. Expect live performance to be materially worse than purged walk-forward, and plan to retrain/re-validate continuously.
- Directional scalping vs. market making: market making has a positive structural expectation (spread + rebate) offset by adverse selection and inventory risk; directional scalping has negative structural expectation (pays spread) offset only by signal. ML is better evidenced for *reducing adverse selection* in MM (OFI/microprice as a quote-skew input) than for generating directional alpha.

### Gaps
- No reputable source states a canonical "51–55% directional accuracy" figure for short horizons; the figure circulates in practitioner folklore but could not be cited.
- No published, audited live track record of a retail ML scalping bot (positive or negative) was found; evidence on failure rates is anecdotal or unverified.

## Q8. What quant firms actually do at short horizons, why retail cannot compete on latency, and what niches remain

### Takeaway
At seconds-and-below horizons institutional P&L comes from market making, latency arbitrage and statistical arbitrage executed in microseconds with colocated, FPGA-class infrastructure; the FCA/QJE evidence that races are won in 5–10 µs and are worth ~half a tick makes it quantitatively impossible for a retail bot with millisecond-plus latency to compete there. Remaining retail niches are slower, structurally different games: crypto microstructure at 100 ms–minutes scale, session-specific and event-driven effects, less-crowded instruments, and passive liquidity provision where rebates and careful adverse-selection filtering can cover costs.

### Cited Findings
- Aquilina, Budish & O'Neill (QJE 2022 / FCA OP50): ~1 race per minute per FTSE-100 symbol; modal duration 5–10 µs, mean ~79–81 µs; each race worth ~half a tick; aggregate "latency arbitrage tax" ≈ $5bn/yr globally and a 17% liquidity-cost reduction if eliminated; the FIA EPTA industry rebuttal argues the methodology overstates races and that remaining races are only 20–30% as profitable. **[peer-reviewed; industry rebuttal]** — [FCA OP50](https://www.fca.org.uk/publications/occasional-papers/occasional-paper-no-50-quantifying-high-frequency-trading-arms-race-new-methodology); [NBER digest](https://www.nber.org/digest/202110/race-exploit-stock-price-differences-between-exchanges); [FIA EPTA review](https://www.fia.org/sites/default/files/2020-05/20200430_FIA%20EPTA%20Review_FCA%20Occasional%20Paper_0.pdf)
- Kolm & Westray were named Risk.net "Buy-side quants of the year" for the deep-OFI work, indicating the institutional relevance of order-flow modelling for execution/market making rather than retail alpha. **[industry press]** — [Risk.net](https://www.risk.net/7962610)
- Oxford-Man / JAX-LOB and the Nagy et al. execution agent show the institutional-academic RL agenda is about *execution* of an existing signal on a simulator, not retail signal generation. **[preprint]** — [JAX-LOB](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2308.13289); [Synced on Nagy et al.](https://syncedreview.com/2023/01/25/oxford-us-deep-double-duelling-q-learning-translates-trading-signals-into-sota-trading-strategies/amp/)
- Crypto: Binance public depth diffs update at 100 ms or 1000 ms, and the published book excludes RPI orders. **[vendor docs]** — [Binance docs](https://developers.binance.com/docs/derivatives/usds-margined-futures/websocket-market-streams/How-to-manage-a-local-order-book-correctly)
- Market-maker economics in crypto: exchanges and issuers spend ~$1.2bn/yr paying market makers; bilateral MM contracts are susceptible to adverse selection (Hummingbot paper); a 2025 study on Binance BTC perps formalises the fill-probability vs. adverse-selection trade-off. **[practitioner / preprint]** — [Hummingbot](https://docs.hummingbot.org/liquidity-mining.pdf); [arXiv 2502.18625](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.18625v2)
- Kolm et al. / Lucchese et al.: predictability is strongest in "information-rich" names with many updates per price change and weakest in illiquid names — so the *most* predictable instruments are exactly where HFT competition is fiercest. **[peer-reviewed]** — [Quantpedia summary](https://quantpedia.com/how-to-use-deep-order-flow-imbalance/); [arXiv 2211.13777](https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.13777v3)

### Inferences
- Latency arithmetic: if the winning race is decided in ~10 µs and a retail cloud box is 10–100 ms from the matching engine, the retail bot is 10³–10⁴× too slow to capture any order-anticipation/latency-arb edge. Any apparent edge in a retail backtest that assumes execution at the observed quote is probably this phantom edge.
- Viable retail niches (inferred from the structure above, not from a paper demonstrating retail profitability):
  1. **Crypto perps at 100 ms–5 min horizons** — free L2 data, 100 ms public snapshots mean everyone on the public feed sees the same state, fees are explicit, and maker rebates exist on some venues/tiers; the competition is still fierce but not FPGA-bound.
  2. **Passive liquidity provision with ML adverse-selection gating** — use OFI/microprice/trade-flow to pull or skew quotes when flow looks toxic; this is the one place the Q1 evidence maps directly onto a positive-expectation strategy.
  3. **Session/event structure** — open/close auctions, funding-rate timestamps, scheduled macro releases, thin Asian-session hours; ML as a filter on a rule that only trades those windows.
  4. **Less-efficient / smaller instruments** — but note the evidence that sparse books degrade flow-feature predictability (Briola/HLOB), so "less efficient" also means "less predictable" with these features.
- What retail should *not* attempt: cross-venue latency arb, order anticipation on US large-caps, sub-100 ms take-the-spread scalping on equities, or any strategy whose backtest edge disappears when a 200 ms execution delay is added.

### Gaps
- No public, verifiable description of a specific HFT firm's current short-horizon ML stack was found; the "what firms do" picture is assembled from academic execution/MM research, the FCA race study and industry press.
- No study found measuring realistic retail-achievable latency to Binance/Bybit/Coinbase matching engines, nor one showing a retail MM strategy with positive net P&L; the niche list is structural inference.
